# The Jade Court

## Story draft — v2

King James remembered the clock, the roar of the crowd, and the ball leaving his hand. Then the rim split open like a ring of sunlight.

He landed on polished jade.

A crown rolled down the steps beside him. Hundreds of people knelt. Somewhere behind the throne, a small, frightened man whispered, “Your Majesty, please put that back on. The provincial ambassadors are watching.”

By sunset, James had learned three things. Everyone here knew him as King Poon. Nobody had heard of basketball. And tomorrow, he was expected to defend the crown at a board game he had never played.

The small man, a court clerk named Shen, brought him the rules.

There were nine volumes.

For generations, the Poon dynasty had presided over an empire whose provinces settled their disputes at the Jade Court. After a succession of ruinous wars, their ancestors had agreed to stake their claims on games. Borders, trade privileges, and access to the imperial reservoirs were recorded in tournament ledgers. Every ruler swore to honor the results. A province that broke its oath could lose access to the roads, granaries, and markets of all the others.

The arrangement had kept the peace for a hundred years. Shen said this with pride. He was less eager to explain who chose the stakes.

Four great provinces now held most of the empire's wealth.

High in the **Feathered Peaks**, bridges hung between forested cliffs, and couriers traveled with birds trained to find paths through the mountain fog. The province's people tended nesting grounds and orchards with equal care. Their favored game, *The Thousand Nests*, began with a nearly empty landscape. A seed brought a bird; the bird scattered a fruit; the fruit fed another visitor. By the final round, one careful action could set half the board in motion. Visitors often lost while admiring how beautifully everything worked.

Across the mountains lay the **Amber Expanse**, where cities rose around deep wells and caravans crossed the desert beneath enormous cloth canopies. Water wardens, merchant families, and scouts competed for influence. Their game, *The Veiled Council*, gave each player a few agents and far too many places to send them. A promise could open a gate. A withheld promise could close a kingdom. Soldiers mattered, but so did the person who had quietly purchased their water supply three turns earlier.

Along the western rivers stood the **Burgundy Marches**, a patchwork of vineyards, mines, market towns, and fortified estates. Here, a ruler earned respect by making the land productive. In *The Six Estates*, numbered lots determined which opportunities were available, and players fitted fields, workshops, ships, and castles into their domains. The best players always had another useful move when their first choice vanished. The Marches also maintained the empire's most dependable army. Its soldiers arrived with dry boots, full stomachs, and replacement wheels for their carts.

To the south, the **Living Gardens** surrounded great animal sanctuaries. Scholars shared their cities with keepers, builders, and visitors who traveled for weeks to see creatures found nowhere else. Their game, *The Living Covenant*, asked players to develop a sanctuary while choosing when to build, seek patrons, welcome animals, or support conservation. An action left waiting became stronger, but the opportunity it was meant for might disappear. The province's council argued constantly over the same question: how much public spectacle should pay for work the public would never see?

At their meeting point stood the capital and its jade throne. The crown still commanded the imperial reservoirs and the tournament seal, but its own estates had dwindled. Each province supplied something the capital needed. Each ambassador arrived with advice about how the king should repay that generosity.

Poon recognized the situation immediately. Four stars, one ball, and nobody interested in passing.

He assumed that made him the answer.

His first opponent was Lady Suri of the Amber Expanse. During their match, he pushed agents into the center of the board, secured the most imposing positions, and forced her to retreat twice. The spectators began to cheer. He gave them a small bow.

Suri spent her last turn calling in three agreements he had barely noticed.

Shen turned over the scoring tablets. Poon had lost.

He could accept that. He asked for another game.

Then a clerk carried the reservoir ledger to Suri's table, and Poon learned that he had just conceded a season of water priority. Downstream farming villages would receive whatever remained after the desert caravans took their allotment.

“Those were the blue pieces?” he asked.

Suri looked at him for a long moment. “What did you think they were?”

He ordered Shen to arrange a visit to the affected villages. His ministers objected. Kings traditionally received reports.

The first farmer he met wanted to know whether she should plant.

Poon had no answer. For the first time since arriving, he wished someone would stop calling him Your Majesty.

He postponed the court's celebrations and set out through the provinces, leaving Shen to keep the government functioning and send increasingly exasperated letters. Officially, the journey was preparation for the Great Confluence, the tournament at which the provinces renewed their claims for the coming years. Privately, Poon wanted to understand what he had been moving around those boards.

In the Feathered Peaks, a courier named Lin agreed to teach him. He kept choosing the rarest, most impressive birds. She beat him with a modest orchard full of ordinary ones. Later, on her delivery route, he saw why: the orchard fed birds that protected nearby crops, and those farms maintained the paths her couriers used. When a provincial official proposed clearing the orchard for a grand aviary, Lin refused to carry the announcement.

Poon invited her to join his court. She agreed on the condition that he help stop the clearing first.

In the Amber Expanse, Suri made him accompany a water convoy before she would grant a rematch. At the final well, he watched her divide an inadequate supply among people who all had reasonable claims. Her province had won water because its existing wells were failing. She had known exactly which villages would bear the cost.

He still resented her victory. It became harder to dismiss her reasons.

She offered him a place for a desert adviser in his traveling party: Nadir, a caravan negotiator with an excellent memory and an inconvenient habit of asking what every royal promise would cost. Poon had to guarantee the convoy a safe route in return.

In the Burgundy Marches, the Warlord of Wei welcomed him to a fortress that looked capable of resisting a century-long siege. Wei expected the visiting king to admire its walls. Poon became more interested in Mei, the quartermaster arguing that the fortress had enough stone and needed another grain store.

Mei taught him *The Six Estates* while repairing a washed-out supply road. Whenever the lots frustrated his plans, she asked what he could still accomplish. By their fifth game, he had stopped waiting for the perfect turn. By their sixth, he noticed that Wei's proposed new fortifications would divert labor from river repairs across the entire province.

Wei declined to discuss it. Mei requested leave to accompany the king.

In the Living Gardens, Poon offered to fund a magnificent royal pavilion. A keeper named An took him to see the proposed site: a quiet stretch of riverbank where an endangered animal had finally begun to breed.

He withdrew the proposal. Finding money for the less glamorous work took longer. An needed agreements protecting the animals' migration route beyond the sanctuary walls, including land controlled by the other provinces. She joined his delegation carrying maps that none of his ministers had ever asked to see.

By the time they returned to the capital, Poon had four advisers who disagreed about almost everything. Lin wanted time to cultivate a network. Nadir wanted commitments before anyone acted. Mei wanted to know who would build it. An wanted to know what the building would destroy.

Their first planning session lasted until dawn.

Poon loved it. He also discovered that calling someone a teammate did not automatically make them trust him.

As the Great Confluence approached, reports began to overlap. Birds had abandoned parts of the Peaks. Desert wells were sinking. Barges in the Marches ran aground, while the southern sanctuaries lost their seasonal wetlands. Each provincial court blamed a neighbor.

Spread across one table, the advisers' maps revealed the damage. Years of separately rewarded projects had drawn more water from the same river system: expanded estates, diverted channels, prestigious gardens, new caravan stations. Every ruler could point to a legal claim and a successful tournament record. The river could no longer meet their promises.

Wei arrived at the capital with a proposal. At the Confluence, he would stake his provincial trade privileges against control of the imperial reservoirs. He promised to repair the waterways with his engineers and enforce rationing with his army. Several frightened governors supported him.

Poon's advisers agreed that Wei could deliver the repairs. They also knew how difficult it would be to remove his soldiers afterward.

The king accepted the challenge, then petitioned to enter a delegation under an old team provision in the tournament charter. Wei laughed when he saw the names: a courier, a negotiator, a quartermaster, and an animal keeper.

He stopped laughing during the opening rounds.

The Confluence's great board joined routes, estates, habitats, and councils in a single contest. Poon's delegation made awkward moves that seemed to surrender easy points. Lin established a chain of modest way stations. Nadir negotiated passage between rivals. Mei used those routes to supply repairs far from the capital. An secured the floodplains the repairs would restore. Each move gave someone else at the table a better next turn.

Wei remained ahead. His fortresses and stored resources had put him within reach of victory.

In the final round, Poon could take a rich river estate and keep his own bid for reservoir control alive. The audience saw it. So did Wei.

Mei pointed to an unfinished channel. An laid her migration map beside it. Nadir checked the terms of their agreements. Lin waited.

Poon spent his turn opening the channel.

The move gave up his personal claim. It also connected the delegation's work, allowing them to meet the charter's joint stewardship condition. Under the terms filed before play, the reservoirs would pass to a council of the participating provinces instead of a single ruler. Their combined standing narrowly surpassed Wei's.

There was no burst of magic to fill the river. Outside, the drought continued. The verdict gave them authority to begin work, and a room full of powerful people with reasons to obstruct it.

Wei demanded the place on the council owed to the Marches. Poon granted it. They needed his engineers.

Suri demanded that the desert's minimum water supply be written down before she signed. An insisted on protections for the wetlands. The downstream villages sent their own representative, who asked why anyone should have to win a tournament to be heard.

This time, Poon had an answer: they shouldn't.

Changing the charter would take more than one victory. For now, he secured a protected minimum supply for drinking water and essential crops, outside future tournament stakes. Then he opened the council's proceedings to the people whose livelihoods filled its ledgers.

Weeks later, he stood beside the first repaired sluice as water reached a dry irrigation ditch. The farmer he had met after his defeat was there. She watched the flow, tested the soil with her heel, and told him they were late, but she could still plant.

That evening, Poon had a wooden hoop fixed above a courtyard gate. His advisers took turns arguing about its height. Shen arrived with a ball, six urgent petitions, and a letter recovered from a sealed room beneath the throne.

On the letter was a drawing of a circle splitting open above a jade floor.

Poon set down the ball.

## Development references

The provincial games are fictional. Their creative foundations are *Wingspan* for the Feathered Peaks, *Dune* for the Amber Expanse, *The Castles of Burgundy* for the Burgundy Marches, and *Ark Nova* for the Living Gardens. The Dune inspiration remains broad until a particular game or edition is chosen. These are story inspirations rather than exact adaptations of published rules.
